Australia's changing population

Australia Is Ageing But Not Everywhere at the Same Pace

Australia has grown dramatically older over the past half-century, but demographic change is unfolding very differently across communities.

01

A nation transformed

Australia has grown older

The share of Australians aged 65 and over has more than doubled since 1971, while the oldest age groups have grown even faster.

Older Australians now make up a much larger share of the population

Share of Australia's population aged 65+ and 85+, 1971–2025.

The shift is already substantial. The share of Australians aged 65+ rose from 8.3% in 1971 to 17.5% in 2025, while the 85+ share increased from 0.5% to 2.2%.

Australia's population has become visibly older

Population by age group and sex, comparing 1971 with 2025.

The change is structural. Compared with 1971, Australia's 2025 population has a broader older-age profile and a narrower base.

Ageing isn't evenly distributed

Where is Australia oldest today?

Some regional and coastal communities have vastly higher shares of older residents than Australia's largest cities.

Older Australia is concentrated far beyond the major cities

Share of each Local Government Area's population aged 65 and over, 2025.

Ageing is geographically uneven. Higher 65+ shares occur across many regional and coastal LGAs, while numerous metropolitan LGAs have lower shares.

Older populations cluster in particular communities

LGAs with the highest share of residents aged 65+, 2025.

Victor Harbor leads the ranking. Its 65+ share reaches 43.0%, followed by Glamorgan–Spring Bay at 38.1% and Yorke Peninsula at 37.5%. Only LGAs with at least 5,000 residents are included.

03

Share is only half the story

Oldest doesn't always mean most

Communities with the highest proportion of older residents are not necessarily the places containing the largest older populations.

The largest older populations are concentrated around major cities

Number of residents aged 65 and over by Local Government Area, 2025. Circle area represents population size.

Large populations create large counts. Brisbane contains more than 183,000 residents aged 65+ despite only 13.3% of its population belonging to this age group. Select a state above to inspect dense metropolitan areas.

High shares do not always mean large older populations

Each point represents an LGA with at least 5,000 residents. Compare the share aged 65+ with the number of older residents. The population axis uses a logarithmic scale.

Share and scale are related, but not interchangeable. Victor Harbor sits at the high-share end of the distribution, while Brisbane stands out for population size. The logarithmic vertical scale keeps both small and large LGAs visible.

The largest older populations have different age profiles

Age composition of the ten LGAs with the largest populations aged 65+, 2025. Cells show each age group's share of the LGA's population aged 65+.

The largest older populations still differ internally. Among these ten LGAs, the 85+ share of the older population ranges from 9.3% in Logan to 15.0% in Canterbury–Bankstown.

04

Change has been highly uneven

Some communities are ageing much faster

Australia's national ageing trend hides major differences in how individual communities have changed since 2001.

Some LGAs have aged dramatically in just 24 years

Percentage-point change in the share of residents aged 65 and over, 2001–2025.

Local ageing has been highly uneven. Many regional and coastal LGAs recorded substantial increases in their 65+ share between 2001 and 2025, while a smaller number of metropolitan areas moved in the opposite direction.

The national trend hides communities moving in opposite directions

Selected LGAs showing their share of residents aged 65+ in 2001 and 2025.

Some communities moved in opposite directions. Toodyay and Glamorgan–Spring Bay gained about 19 percentage points in their 65+ shares, while Melbourne's fell from 8.4% to 6.3%.

A national picture, different state profiles

Ageing looks different across the country

Local changes add up to different population profiles across Australia's states and territories.

Each state tells a different story of population ageing

Share of residents aged 65+ and number of older residents, 2025. Tiles are schematic, not geographic boundaries.

State percentages can hide very different population scales. Tasmania has the highest 65+ share among the states, while NSW contains the largest older population. Other Territories has a high percentage but only about 1,150 older residents.

Looking ahead

The shift isn't over

Australia's population is projected to continue ageing, increasing the importance of planning for older populations and the services that support them.

Australia's population is projected to keep ageing

Share of Australia's population aged 65+ and 85+, observed 1971–2025 and projected 2026–2071 under the ABS low-series scenario.

The shift is projected to continue. Under the ABS low-series scenario, the 65+ share reaches 27.3% by 2071 and the 85+ share reaches 5.8%. Dashed lines represent projections rather than observed outcomes.

The distributions are similar, but not identical. Queensland's share of residential-care users is 1.29 percentage points above its share of Australians aged 85+, while NSW is 0.75 points below. This compares service locations with population distribution, not access or unmet need.

07

The bigger picture

A national shift, with local realities

Australia is ageing, but national averages conceal very different experiences across communities.

The share of Australians aged 65+ has more than doubled since 1971 and is projected to keep rising. Yet ageing is distributed unevenly: regional and coastal communities often have the highest proportions of older residents, while major cities contain the largest numbers.

Some communities have aged rapidly since 2001 while others have moved in the opposite direction. These differences matter because percentages alone cannot show how many older people live in a place, and national trends cannot reveal how quickly individual communities are changing.

Australia's ageing is national, but its local realities differ.
